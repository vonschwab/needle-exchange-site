The Needle Exchange

I have been building something. Two things at once: a listening based music news & discovery feed that replaces streaming algorithms, and a replacement for the Spotify collaborative playlist. Here's what it is, what already works, and how you could help if any of it sounds fun.

One

What it is

Start here

You remember the monthly playlist? We each posted five tracks every month. It wasn't a competition or a performance. Just a place to earnestly post what we were actually listening to. That was a pretty great music recommendation system, and it died when most of us left Spotify.

This started as me trying to recreate that, but I got obsessed and accidentally turned it into something slightly more ambitious.

Why bother

Leaving Spotify was the right move

Spotify had a genuinely good idea: use what somebody actually listens to to predict what they might want next. Then that idea got corrupted through a bunch of predictable corporate bullshit — pay-to-play playlist slots, blackbox algorithm manipulation, AI-generated filler, all while paying artists virtually nothing and sending their money to the military industrial complex. Absolutely repulsive stuff.

Let's start with the same idea, except that we don't let it get all fucked up. I built an app that reads what you actually play (via Last.fm), works out which artists and labels matter to you, subscribes to them across the web (Bandcamp and YouTube, for now) and tells you when something new lands. It doesn't host any music. It just points you at new music.

An important data rule: every single recommendation can say why it's there. Not in a vague "because you listened to X" way, but an actual provenance trail you can open and read.
The feed

This isn't Letterboxd for music

No homework. Yes, there's a taste profile. But, you never manually fill it in. No list to curate, no slider, nothing to rate out of five, no artist you have to remember to go and add; It's built from what you actually played — your Last.fm scrobbles, plus whatever listening history you feel like importing.

The app automatically works out which artists are worth watching, follows their Bandcamp and YouTube, and subscribes to them for you. They post a single? We tell you. A label that you obviously trust signs a new artist? It shows up in your discovery feed. Somebody announces an album? It's on your release calendar.

You can still edit your own feed — hide a release, mute an artist you no longer care about, unfollow a label that isn't for you, pick which platform you'd rather listen on, etc.

A digestible feed

No more deluge of endless recommendations. The front page is something you can finish. Five channels — new albums, new singles, videos and live sessions, a release calendar, and a discovery shelf, all ranked by your own listening habits. Each channel is a playable queue. You get through it, you're done, go do something else.

Still want more? There is a separate infinite scroll for the real freaks.

The playlist

The monthly mix, streaming agnostic

The other half is the one you already know, because we ran it for years by hand. One playlist per month per group. Opens on the 1st, closes at month's end, and freezes into something permanent — March 2026, there forever, with everyone's picks under their own names. Two people picking the same track isn't a collision, it's corroboration, and you both show up.

Cross-platform this time, so nobody's locked out: your pick is a record, not a Spotify URI, and everyone opens it wherever they actually listen.

No comments, no votes, no ranking — and nothing that counts who played your song, who skipped it, or who contributed the most popular pick. It's collaborative selection, not a competition. This is the first social piece I want to build, because we already know it works.

Anti-social media

The site has a social component, but not like that. This platform is built for zero pressure. It is philosophically not performative: no public feed, no panopticon.

None of the social media machinery: no follower counts, friend counts, no streaks, no leaderboards, no contribution scores, no popularity badges, no "people you may know." The social graph exists internally where a feature needs it to work. The graph is never the content.

No free text anywhere.

No comments, no captions, no DMs, no threads, no reviews. No chat, ever. You want to chat about something? Send a text.

A limited amount of social interactions, all focusing on music:

Record recommendations, where the record is the whole message

You send it. That's the message. No note.

And that's it. You don't find out whether they opened it, played it, saved it, binned it, or never saw it because they'd quietly muted the group it came through. Sending music should mean I thought you might like this — not assigning someone homework and then checking whether they did it. You want to tell them you liked it? That's what phones, texting, and group chats are for.

It's music sharing without performance.
Two kinds
of relationship

There are exactly two sorts of social relationships on the site, they're different things, and you can have either, both, or neither with any given person.

The club
cont.

Nobody is getting their feelings hurt. You can leave without leaving

If a group stops being for you, you mute it. You're still listed as a member to everyone else, nobody gets a notification, and nobody can tell it's muted. It just stops asking for your attention — recommendations through it silently don't arrive. From the outside, nothing happened.

And when you come back six months later, you land in the group as it is now. No pile of homework you missed.

The rules

Things it will never do

✕
No model writes anything you read about a record.

Every caption, and every explanation of why something turned up, is assembled by templates out of database rows. No AI generated descriptions, no summaries, no headlines, no "why this" blurb, no invented comparisons between bands. If a fact isn't in the database, that part of the sentence just doesn't appear.

✕
It never decides what's good.

There is an algorithm that ranks your listening that measures directly against your real listening history. How much you've played an artist, how recently, how consistently. That's it.

✕
No black box.

The score is stored with its parts broken out, and the caption under every card exists specifically so you can poke at it. If it can't say why something reached you, it doesn't reach you.

✕
It won't manufacture your friendships.

No friend suggestions, no group directory, no "people you may know," no graph-based discovery of any kind. Relationships come from outside the app — you already know these people — and the app's job is to support that, not to generate it. The social graph is never mined to grow the social graph.

OK, the AI question

Yes, there's a language model in here. It acts like a neutral data adjudicator. It is limited to the following actions: It can work out which known release a candidate is; research what genre something is; work out whether a label's catalogue is actually curated - Western Vinyl = Yes, Warner Bros = No; and work out whether two spellings are the same band.

That's it. Four bits of boring librarian work, each one producing a typed answer attached to its evidence, each one something I can overrule by hand in a review queue. It's never asked what's good. Never asked what to recommend. Never asked to write a word. It doesn't even find anything — every release in the feed was turned up by a dumb connector checking a subscribed artist's page and reading structured data off it.

There's a test that fails the build if one of those connectors so much as imports the model. That wall is the thing I'd least like to see quietly erode, so I made it noisy.

Why it matters

That table is constructed from three separate systems:

One shared database of records. Finding and identifying new releases happens once, for everybody. Your feed is your own view over it, ranked by your own listening. That's why Jeff had a working front page for weeks before he had a single subscription of his own — my subscriptions were bringing in the records, and his listening ranked them.

Automatic subscriptions. Nobody picks artists by hand. The system reads what you've played and subscribes for you. The first time it ran for Jeff it subscribed to 144 artists. It has run every night since; he's at 1,169 now, and I'm at 3,426. If you unsubscribe from an artist, that's permanent — nothing will ever quietly re-add them.

Artist identity from MusicBrainz. Last weekend the system asked MusicBrainz — the open music encyclopedia — about all 5,894 artists we hold an identifier for, and cached the answers: official Bandcamp pages, YouTube channels, discographies. So a subscription attaches to an artist's actual page, not a name search's best guess. It also settled a real question: about a thousand of the artists we couldn't find on Bandcamp simply aren't on Bandcamp. Now we know, instead of assuming.

Still missing before anyone else joins: a login, real hosting, and proof the costs stay sane with more than two people.

The videos column is zero for both of us because video support isn't built yet. The page says so, rather than padding itself out.

Four

If you want in

Pick one

There's no wrong size of involvement here. Genuinely — "I'll use it and complain" is a real contribution and probably the one I need most.

Just use it

Give me a Last.fm account and be a third person in the system. Every number in here is tuned against two record collections, which is not many. A third one that looks nothing like ours is how I find out what I've accidentally built around my own taste. This is the easiest ask and possibly the most useful.

Tell me it's wrong

Look at your feed and tell me what's stupid about it. Why is that on there? Why isn't this? That's a real job, it needs zero code, and you all have better ears than I do. Half the good decisions in here came from someone saying "that looks off" about a specific record.

Build the front end

Biggest single unlock by a distance. The server side is finished and typed; the page you'd actually look at isn't. If you like making dense, information-heavy things feel easy, you'd move this further in a couple of weekends than anything else on the list. There's a look sketched out — you're reading it — but it's a first instinct, not a decision. Argue with it.

Get it hosted

It runs on my laptop. It needs to run somewhere real: a database, a Python API, a Next.js app, one nightly job. Nothing exotic, but it's nobody's job right now and it blocks everything else.

Nerd out on the data

The genuinely fun problems in here are identity problems. Is "Avalon Emerson & the Charm" the same act as "Avalon Emerson"? Is this Topic-channel upload a real release or noise? These get settled by measuring something, and every answer gets written down. The maths section below is for you.

Just be interested

Honestly, telling me this is worth finishing is worth something. I've been at it a while. If your only involvement is getting the monthly playlist going again on a thing I built, that's a completely good outcome and the reason it exists.

Fair warning

How I've been working on it

Four habits, each one the result of screwing something up:

  • Numbers get measured, not remembered. There's a script for it, and quoting a figure without running it counts as a mistake. This came from a day I lost to the same data being "47% covered" and "92.4% covered" depending on what you divided by — both true, one of them terrifying and completely wrong for the decision I was making.
  • If something's missing, go check the source. An empty field in my database tells you about my code, not about the world. Before anyone says "the API doesn't give us that," they go make the API call.
  • Nothing's done until it's switched on and checked against real data. Tests passing and a page rendering correctly are not, on their own, worth anything.
  • The database is protected by actual controls, not by me being careful. One command I didn't review properly wrote 2,429 junk rows into a queue that had one real thing in it. The fix wasn't a sterner note to self — there was already a note to self. It was a snapshot wrapper and a hard block on the commands that write.

That probably reads as a lot. It's actually the opposite in practice: nearly every hard call has a dated write-up saying what I tried and what I measured, so you can disagree with any of it on the merits instead of guessing what I was thinking. There are 52 of those.

Six

The actual maths

For the
data guys

Since a few of you will want to argue with this: here are the real formulas, copied out of the code, weights and all. If the ranking is supposed to be inspectable, that has to include being inspectable by you.

Step one — how much do you care about an artist

Every artist you've played gets a score in [0, 1]. Three terms, fixed weights:

# gate: fewer than 6 plays and you don't exist to the model at all if effective_plays < 6: return 0.0 # each term is normalised to YOUR OWN maximum, not a global one magnitude(n, n_max) = min( log1p(n) / log1p(n_max), 1.0 ) affinity = 0.60 · lifetime + 0.25 · repeat_breadth + 0.15 · recent_interest # and a flat floor if you own it in your library if owned: affinity = max(affinity, 0.10)
apps/api/app/taste/affinity.py
0.60
lifetime — magnitude(total_plays, your_biggest_artist). Log-scaled, so going from 10 plays to 100 matters far more than 1,000 to 1,090.
0.25
repeat breadth — did you come back to them? Last.fm: active_weeks / max_active_weeks over a 104-week window. Spotify: 0.70·log(years)/log(10) + 0.30·log(months)/log(36). One period earns nothing — you have to return.
0.15
recent interest — absolute recent plays, normalised the same way. Deliberately not a share of your lifetime listening, so a heavy old favourite isn't punished for you having moved on this month.

Two choices worth flagging

Providers are fused with max(), never added. Your Spotify streams and your Last.fm scrobbles are frequently the same play counted twice. Each provider is normalised independently and then the components are combined by taking the larger — adding them would double-count anyone who ran both.

Everything is normalised to your own ceiling. There's no global play-count scale, so someone with 200,000 scrobbles and someone with 4,000 get the same spread of scores. It's a rank over your listening, not a measure of how much you listen.

And the honest cost of that 6-play gate: on real data it zeroes 73 of 449 Last.fm signals — about 16%. An artist you just started playing stays invisible until the sixth play. That price is paid entirely by new-artist discovery, which is the half of this model that needs the most work.

Step two — what order things appear in

Artist affinity is one of five terms in the score that actually sorts your feed:

relevance = 0.55 · artist_affinity + 0.15 · label_trust + 0.10 · genre_affinity + 0.10 · event_weight + 0.10 · corroboration corroboration = min( log10(mentions + 1) / log10(6), 1.0 ) event_weight = { released 1.0 · reissued 0.8 · announced 0.7 single 0.5 · concert 0.4 · artist-only 0.3 } # no genre data is NOT the same as bad genre match genre_affinity = 0.5 if unknown else measured_value
apps/api/app/ranking/relevance.py

That 0.5 neutral is a real distinction, not a fudge. "We've never classified this record" and "we classified it and you don't like this genre" are different facts, and collapsing them would either punish everything we haven't got round to tagging or flatter genres you've shown no interest in.

The part I'd want you to poke at

At a weight of 0.55, artist_affinity is structurally 0.0 for any artist you've never played. So a total stranger's maximum possible relevance is 0.45, no matter how good the record is or how much you trust the label.

That's deliberate — I wanted a proven listening signal to beat a label-backed unknown — but it means this blend does not serve new-artist discovery at all, and I know it. When I last measured, the best-placed unknown artist sat at rank 41 of 45, with none in the top ten. Fixing that properly is open work, and it's the bit I'd most like someone to have opinions about.

Step three — how much evidence is behind it

Separately from the score, every artist you've played lands in a band, and the bands are what let a card say how strongly it knows something:

Established
Above the crossover rank. Sustained listening; the evidence stands on its own.
Emerging
Below the crossover, but artists you are established on point at them. Light listening, corroborated.
Inferred
Never played at all. Ranked on a different scale entirely, and can only leave this band by you actually playing them — being surfaced never promotes anything, or the system's guesses become its own evidence.

The crossover isn't a constant. It's found per person by walking down your ranked artists and asking where corroboration falls off. Two details from building it:

  • Corroboration is boolean, not counted. We measured that the vote count is noise across a thirtyfold range, while having any vote does real work. So it's "does anything point here," not "how many things."
  • There is no play-count floor on Emerging, on purpose. One play plus corroboration is genuinely weak evidence, and the honest way to say that is to rank it last — not to invent a threshold that looks rigorous and isn't.

And one thing I'm oddly proud of: the corroboration step costs API calls, so it's budgeted. If the budget — rather than your data — is what determined where the crossover landed, the whole run refuses and reports nothing instead of returning a number that's really an artifact of a cost limit. That bug nearly shipped once.

Every one of these numbers is a constant in a file with a comment explaining what it was calibrated against and what it costs. None of them are tuned per-user, none of them move on their own, and no model has ever touched any of them.
